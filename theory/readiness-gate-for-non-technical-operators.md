# Readiness Gate Intern Guide for Non-Technical Operators

This guide explains why AI plans often look impressive and then fail in real execution. It is written for interns, operators, project managers, founders, assistants, and non-technical team members who need to use AI for serious planning without getting fooled by confident nonsense.

The core rule is simple:

Do not ask AI for a plan first. Ask whether a plan is allowed.

A plan is allowed only when the necessary context, resources, field truth, options, risks, and stop rules are visible enough to support execution. If those ingredients are missing, the correct output is not a timeline. The correct output is preparation, discovery, or a small test.

## One-Page Reminder

Use this section before any AI-generated plan, roadmap, website build, coding plan, business launch, outreach campaign, hiring plan, fitness plan, nutrition plan, or agent workflow.

### The One Rule

No normal plan first. Run the Readiness Gate first.

### The Fast Checklist

1. **Decision:** What decision must this plan support?
2. **Context:** What do we know, and what is missing?
3. **Resources:** What time, money, people, tools, data, assets, skills, access, and relationships are actually available?
4. **Field truth:** What has been checked with real people, real users, real buyers, logs, tests, operations, field visits, or expert review?
5. **Preparation:** What must exist before execution starts?
6. **Minimum viable preparedness:** What is the minimum preparation that reduces the major risks without creating research theater?
7. **Options:** What cheap Plan B, Plan C, or Plan D should we prepare so we are not trapped by one fragile plan?
8. **Red team:** If this plan failed, what probably caused the failure?
9. **Smallest reality test:** What is the smallest action that creates real evidence?
10. **Gate:** What evidence means continue, pivot, pause, or kill?
11. **Stop loss:** When do we stop spending time, money, attention, reputation, or energy?
12. **Reinvestment:** What artifact, template, data, contact, skill, workflow, or proof should be saved so the next loop gets faster?

### Readiness Levels

| Level | Meaning | Correct output |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Goal, resources, or field truth are missing. | Discovery or context packet. |
| Orange | Some ingredients exist, but execution would rely on guessing. | Preparation plan. |
| Yellow | Enough exists for one controlled test, not a full rollout. | Smallest reality test. |
| Green | Major requirements, evidence, resources, and gates are clear. | Execution plan with checkpoints. |

### The Sentence to Remember

A plan without context, resources, field truth, options, red teaming, metrics, and stop loss is not a plan. It is AI theater.

## 1. Why AI Plans Fail

AI plans usually fail because the model begins at the wrong altitude. The user asks for a 12-week plan, a website plan, a business plan, a fitness plan, a coding plan, or an outreach plan. The AI tries to be helpful, so it produces a clean sequence of tasks. The plan has weeks, bullets, phases, and sometimes metrics. It looks serious.

But seriousness is not the same as readiness.

The missing question is: ready for what, with which resources, under which constraints, based on which evidence?

Most bad plans are not bad because the AI is stupid. They are bad because the AI is doing exactly what it was shaped to do: satisfy the request. If the human asks for a plan without giving the real operating context, the model often fills gaps with generic assumptions. It may assume there is a budget. It may assume there is time. It may assume the user can call people, has a target list, knows the buyer, has a calendar, has proof, has the right equipment, has access to the codebase, understands the domain, or can execute consistently.

The human also contributes to the failure. Humans often ask for a plan when they actually need diagnosis, intake, field research, resource inventory, or a decision frame. The human may be tired, frustrated, excited, or impatient. The human may want the feeling of progress. A plan gives that feeling fast. But fast emotional relief can become slow operational failure.

This is why blaming only AI is too easy. Blaming only the human is also too easy. The real failure is the loop:

1. The human asks for execution before readiness is known.
2. AI rewards the request with a plausible plan.
3. The plan hides unknowns inside neat steps.
4. The human starts executing and hits reality.
5. Reality exposes missing ingredients.
6. The human loses trust in the plan, the AI, or themselves.

The Readiness Gate exists to break that loop.

## 2. The Planning Fallacy and the Fake Calendar

The planning fallacy is the tendency to underestimate time, cost, friction, and risk. The Project Management Institute discusses how projects often rely too much on the inside view, where people focus on their own desired scenario instead of comparing against reality and comparable past work: https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/2019/04/07/15/25/planning-fallacy-causes-solutions-project-expectations-6374

AI makes the planning fallacy easier to produce because it can create a beautiful timeline instantly. A human can say, "Give me an 8-week launch plan," and the AI can produce one in seconds. That speed is dangerous when the plan is not grounded.

A fake calendar usually says things like:

- Week 1: define the offer.
- Week 2: build the website.
- Week 3: start outreach.
- Week 4: book calls.
- Week 5: close customers.

This looks fine until you ask:

- Who is the buyer?
- What pain has been validated?
- What proof exists?
- What list exists?
- What channel works?
- What budget exists?
- Who writes the copy?
- Who takes calls?
- What happens if nobody replies?
- What is the stop loss?

If those questions are unanswered, the plan is not wrong in the abstract. It is worse: it is unearned. It pretends execution is ready when the ingredients have not been prepared.

## 3. The Personal Trainer Analogy

Fitness is one of the easiest ways to understand why generic AI plans fail.

A generic AI fitness plan might say:

- Train five days per week.
- Do upper body on Monday and Thursday.
- Do lower body on Tuesday and Friday.
- Eat high protein.
- Sleep eight hours.
- Track calories.

That advice is not necessarily false. The problem is that it ignores the person.

A serious personal trainer asks:

- What is your goal: fat loss, strength, muscle, mobility, health, sport performance, confidence, pain reduction?
- What is your training history?
- What equipment do you have?
- Do you have injuries?
- How many days can you actually train?
- What time of day works?
- What food do you already eat?
- Who cooks?
- What is your budget?
- What causes you to quit?
- What happens when you travel?
- What is the fallback workout when you only have 20 minutes?
- What is the fallback meal when you are tired?

The difference is not that the personal trainer knows a magic exercise. The difference is assessment, constraints, adherence, feedback, and fallback options.

Nutrition is even clearer. A generic nutrition plan may be technically correct and still fail because the person cannot shop that way, cook that way, afford that way, digest that way, or repeat that way. The plan may ignore culture, family, stress, work schedule, cravings, social events, travel, and emotional eating. The plan may be optimal on paper and impossible in life.

This is the same problem with business, websites, code, hiring, and operations. A plan that ignores the operator is not a real plan. It is a template wearing a deadline.

## 4. The Cooking and Iceberg Model

Execution is the visible part. Preparation is the hidden part.

In a restaurant, customers see fast service. They do not see the prep: buying ingredients, washing, cutting, separating, measuring, labeling, cleaning, staffing, scheduling, testing recipes, and preparing backup stock. The visible speed exists because of invisible preparation.

That is the iceberg method. The public sees the visible move. The advantage is below the surface:

- the context packet,
- the field notes,
- the target list,
- the tested script,
- the asset inventory,
- the fallback path,
- the red team notes,
- the gate criteria,
- the stop rule,
- the reusable template.

The goal is not to make preparation slow. The goal is to make preparation sharp. Think of it as quiet, fast, disciplined prep. It should be invisible to the market or audience, but not invisible to you. Invisible to the outside is useful. Invisible to the operator is dangerous.

AI agents can help create this hidden preparation layer. They can summarize, compare, draft, classify, extract, search, build lists, prepare briefs, run checks, and create artifacts. But if the preparation is not logged, labeled, and evaluated, the hidden layer becomes a black box. A black box is not preparedness. It is risk with better formatting.

## 5. Minimum Viable Preparedness

Minimum viable preparedness means preparing enough to reduce the major risks and enable a real test, without using preparation as an excuse to avoid action.

This is the missing middle between two bad extremes.

Under-preparation looks like this:

- "Let's just launch."
- "We can figure it out later."
- "The AI made the plan, so it should work."
- "We do not need field validation."
- "No blockers."
- "I will improvise."

Over-preparation looks like this:

- "I need two more weeks of research."
- "We need a perfect dashboard first."
- "We need the whole brand system before one buyer call."
- "We need to automate everything before doing one manual test."
- "We need ten fallback plans before trying Plan A."

Minimum viable preparedness asks a better question:

What is the smallest preparation package that lets us test reality without unacceptable risk?

For a website, that might be one clear offer, one target visitor, one proof point, one CTA, and one test section.

For coding, that might be one acceptance criterion, one failing test, one likely file map, and one rollback idea.

For business, that might be one buyer hypothesis, one painful problem, one outreach script, one list of 20 targets, and one stop rule.

For fitness, that might be a three-day plan, a fallback 20-minute workout, a food baseline, and a weekly check-in.

Minimum viable preparedness is not laziness. It is disciplined risk reduction.

## 6. Plan A, B, C, and D Without Wasting Resources

Fallback options are not a sign that the plan is weak. They are a sign that the operator understands uncertainty.

But options cost resources. Too many options become waste. The goal is to buy just enough optionality to avoid being trapped.

Use this model:

| Option | Purpose | Cost rule |
|---|---|---|
| Plan A | The main path if readiness is strong. | Gets most attention only after gate passes. |
| Plan B | The closest fallback if one major assumption fails. | Cheap to prepare. |
| Plan C | A smaller scope if resources are lower than expected. | Protects momentum. |
| Plan D | A stop, pause, or learning path if the idea is not ready. | Protects capital and reputation. |

Examples:

Website:

- Plan A: build the full homepage.
- Plan B: test only the hero and CTA first.
- Plan C: build a one-page waitlist instead of the full site.
- Plan D: pause build and run buyer interviews because nobody understands the offer.

Business:

- Plan A: launch paid workshop.
- Plan B: run a free diagnostic call to learn objections.
- Plan C: sell a smaller paid audit.
- Plan D: stop the offer and reposition based on field evidence.

Fitness:

- Plan A: train four days per week.
- Plan B: train three days during busy weeks.
- Plan C: do two 20-minute minimum workouts.
- Plan D: maintain steps and protein only during travel or stress.

Coding:

- Plan A: implement the full feature.
- Plan B: build the narrow path first.
- Plan C: create a spike or prototype.
- Plan D: stop and rewrite the requirement because acceptance criteria are not testable.

The rule is: options should reduce risk, not multiply complexity.

## 7. Red Teaming Without Paranoia

Red teaming means stress-testing a plan before it meets reality. The light version is a premortem: imagine the plan has failed, then ask why. Gary Klein popularized premortem thinking as a way to expose risks before execution: https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem

This does not mean becoming paranoid. It means forcing the obvious failure modes to appear before they become expensive.

Use the minimum red team:

1. Imagine the plan failed.
2. List the five most likely causes.
3. Mark which causes are preventable before execution.
4. Mark which causes require a fallback option.
5. Mark which causes are acceptable improvisation.
6. Convert the top risk into a readiness requirement or small test.

Examples:

Outreach plan failed because:

- The list was wrong.
- The message was generic.
- The offer was unclear.
- The target buyer had no budget.
- Replies came in, but nobody followed up.

The red team response:

- Test 20 contacts before scaling.
- Write three message variants.
- Confirm the offer in one sentence.
- Ask a budget question early.
- Prepare follow-up templates and calendar slots.

Website plan failed because:

- Visitors did not understand the offer.
- Proof was weak.
- CTA was vague.
- Page looked pretty but did not answer objections.
- AI-agent discoverability was ignored.

The red team response:

- Test the hero with target visitors.
- Add proof inventory.
- Make CTA specific.
- Build objection section.
- Create structured content for humans and AI agents.

Red teaming is not a second plan. It is a pressure test that improves the first plan.

## 8. Readiness Nuance: What Blocks Execution and What Can Be Improvised

Not every missing detail blocks execution.

This is important. If every unknown blocks the plan, preparation becomes paralysis. If no unknown blocks the plan, execution becomes chaos.

Separate missing items into three classes:

| Class | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Blocking requirement | Execution should not start without it. | No buyer, no budget, no owner, no test, no permission, no acceptance criteria. |
| Risk reducer | Helpful before scaling, but not always required for a small test. | Better design polish, larger sample size, extra proof, second channel. |
| Improvisable detail | Can be handled during execution without major risk. | Exact email subject, minor copy phrasing, meeting order, visual micro-adjustment. |

For a business idea, buyer pain is blocking. Exact logo is not.

For a website, offer clarity is blocking. The final animation style is not.

For code, acceptance criteria are blocking. The final internal helper name may not be.

For fitness, injury constraints are blocking. Perfect exercise selection is not.

This is the operator's judgment. A good readiness gate does not demand perfect certainty. It demands clarity about which uncertainty matters.

## 9. Human Movement Versus AI Work

AI can prepare many things quickly:

- summarize documents,
- compare options,
- draft scripts,
- build checklists,
- create templates,
- classify evidence,
- prepare interview guides,
- search public information,
- identify missing context,
- generate red team questions,
- produce eval scenarios.

But AI cannot replace every kind of reality contact.

Some work requires humans to move, call, meet, inspect, negotiate, or observe:

- visiting a location,
- asking a buyer a sensitive question,
- reading body language in a sales conversation,
- testing whether someone actually pays,
- checking a local market,
- verifying physical conditions,
- building trust with a partner,
- handling reputation-sensitive outreach,
- making a judgment under social context.

This is where AI agents become proxies, not owners. An agent can prepare the brief, find candidates, create the evidence standard, compare deliverables, and summarize results. But if the task creates legal, financial, privacy, safety, reputation, or client risk, it needs human approval gates.

AI can compress preparation. It should not erase responsibility.

## 10. AI Agents as Proxies and the Need for Evals

As AI agents become more autonomous, they will increasingly act as layers between you and the world. They may prepare tasks, coordinate workflows, send messages, schedule calls, query tools, brief contractors, or collect evidence. In some cases they may help hire humans for local fieldwork or specialist tasks.

That is powerful, but it creates a new risk: delegated action at machine speed.

The more autonomous the agent, the stronger the evaluation layer must be.

Anthropic's writing on building effective agents emphasizes that reliable agent systems often use simple workflows, clear tool use, and evaluation rather than unnecessary complexity: https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents

OpenAI's agent evals guidance points toward evaluating agent behavior against expected outputs and trajectories, not just final text: https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/agent-evals

Anthropic's eval guidance also stresses that agent evaluations should match realistic tasks and failure modes, not toy benchmarks: https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents

For this readiness skill, evals answer:

- Does the agent refuse premature execution plans?
- Does it ask only high-value questions?
- Does it label assumptions?
- Does it separate preparation from execution?
- Does it identify missing field truth?
- Does it propose small tests?
- Does it create Plan B/C/D without overcomplicating?
- Does it red team lightly?
- Does it include stop loss?
- Does it avoid endless research?

If the skill cannot pass those evals, the skill is not reliable.

## 11. Assets and Resources: What Counts

Many plans fail because "resources" are treated too narrowly. People think resources means money. Money matters, but it is only one asset class.

Use this asset map:

| Asset type | Examples | Evidence to attach |
|---|---|---|
| Money | Cash, budget, credit, grant, revenue, savings | Balance, budget approval, user claim, contract. |
| Time | Available hours, calendar slots, deadline flexibility | Calendar, user claim, schedule. |
| People | Team, contractors, advisors, friends, operators | Names, availability, agreement, role. |
| Relationships | Warm leads, partners, clients, mentors, communities | CRM, messages, intros, call notes. |
| Distribution | Newsletter, audience, social account, marketplace, partner channel | Analytics, subscriber count, access proof. |
| Data | CRM, analytics, logs, surveys, transcripts, market notes | File path, dashboard, export, source. |
| Documents | SOPs, briefs, contracts, pitch decks, prior research | Document link, version, owner. |
| Tools | Software, APIs, hardware, workspace, automations | Subscription, credentials, access check. |
| Technical skills | Coding, design, data analysis, automation, security, operations | Portfolio, repo, demonstrated task. |
| Soft skills | sales, negotiation, teaching, writing, leadership, facilitation | Past results, references, observed performance. |
| Proof | testimonials, case studies, screenshots, outcomes, credentials | First-party evidence, permission to use. |
| Templates | scripts, checklists, prompts, briefs, scorecards | File path, prior use, quality score. |
| Workflows | repeatable process, agent skill, checklist, automation | Run history, owner, output examples. |
| Field access | ability to visit, call, interview, inspect, test locally | Contact, permission, location, budget. |

Every important resource claim should have an evidence label. "We have warm leads" is not the same as a CRM export. "I can code this" is not the same as a passing test. "We have a buyer" is not the same as a paid deposit.

Resources become stronger when they compound. A one-time call is useful. A call transcript, objection map, reusable script, and updated buyer profile are compounding assets.

## 12. The Augmented Loop: Consumption to Reinvestment

Your augmented loop is:

1. Consumption
2. Thinking
3. Creation
4. Productizing
5. Monetization
6. Reinvestment

The readiness gate fits this loop because it prevents each stage from pretending the next stage is ready too early.

### Consumption

Consumption means taking in information: articles, videos, calls, customer conversations, market signals, books, docs, code, competitor pages, field observations.

Failure mode: consuming forever without deciding what matters.

Readiness question: what did we learn, from what source, with what evidence label?

Compounding asset: notes, summaries, source map, insight list, contradiction list.

### Thinking

Thinking means synthesis: patterns, assumptions, decision frames, risks, hypotheses, opportunities, constraints.

Failure mode: thinking stays private and cannot be reused.

Readiness question: what decision does this thinking support?

Compounding asset: decision brief, assumption map, red team notes, options map.

### Creation

Creation means producing artifacts: scripts, pages, code, pitch, offer, prototype, guide, checklist, dataset, design, workflow.

Failure mode: artifact is created before intent is clear.

Readiness question: what behavior or evidence should this artifact produce?

Compounding asset: reusable template, working prototype, tested copy, code with tests.

### Productizing

Productizing means turning an artifact into something repeatable and usable by others.

Failure mode: custom work never becomes a system.

Readiness question: what parts can be packaged, repeated, delegated, priced, or taught?

Compounding asset: SOP, skill, offer page, onboarding flow, QA checklist, delivery process.

### Monetization

Monetization means capturing value: sales, retainers, paid tests, deposits, licensing, subscription, project fees.

Failure mode: building without proof that someone will pay or commit.

Readiness question: what is the smallest paid or commitment signal?

Compounding asset: payment proof, objection list, pricing evidence, buyer profile, sales script.

### Reinvestment

Reinvestment is the stage many people forget. It means using the output of one loop to improve the next loop.

Failure mode: every project starts from zero.

Readiness question: what should be saved, improved, delegated, automated, or turned into a skill?

Compounding asset: better data, better relationships, better skill, better template, better workflow, better proof, better distribution.

The readiness gate should always ask: what does this run create that makes the next run easier?

## 13. Evidence Labels and Fact Checking

Evidence labels prevent AI and humans from treating all statements as equal.

Use these labels:

- Verified fact
- Internal document
- User-provided claim
- First-party source
- Third-party source
- Field signal
- Inference
- Opinion
- Unknown
- Weak claim

The important habit is not bureaucracy. The important habit is honesty.

Example:

"We have 50 leads."

This could mean:

- Verified fact: CRM export shows 50 named leads with emails and source.
- User-provided claim: someone said there are 50 leads.
- Inference: based on LinkedIn search, there may be 50 possible leads.
- Weak claim: "I think there are many leads."

Those are different realities.

Fact checking should focus on claims that affect execution. Do not fact-check everything with equal energy. Fact-check the claims that can break the plan:

- budget,
- buyer,
- legal permission,
- access,
- data quality,
- technical feasibility,
- deadline,
- availability,
- demand,
- proof,
- safety,
- reputation risk.

If a claim can change the decision, label it and verify it.

## 14. Examples of What Goes Wrong

### Example 1: Business Plan

Bad prompt: "Give me a 12-week plan to launch an AI consulting business."

Bad AI answer: A weekly plan with branding, website, outreach, content, calls, proposals, and scaling.

What goes wrong:

- No buyer segment is validated.
- No painful problem is confirmed.
- No proof exists.
- No list exists.
- No channel is chosen.
- No time budget is checked.
- No paid signal is required.
- No stop loss exists.

Readiness Gate answer:

- Decision: should we test this offer with real buyers?
- Preparation: define target buyer, one pain, one offer, one proof point, one outreach script, one list.
- Smallest test: contact 20 qualified buyers and ask for a call or paid diagnostic.
- Gate: continue if a defined number of qualified buyers reply or commit.
- Stop loss: pause or reposition if zero qualified signals after the test.

### Example 2: Website Build

Bad prompt: "Build me a premium website."

What goes wrong:

- The site looks good but says nothing specific.
- The visitor cannot explain the offer.
- The CTA is vague.
- Proof is weak.
- The page is designed before the business decision is clear.

Readiness Gate answer:

- Decision: what must the visitor believe or do?
- Preparation: ICP, offer, proof, objections, CTA, assets, public/private boundaries.
- Smallest test: show the hero section to target visitors and ask what they think the company does.
- Gate: build full page only if the message is understood.

### Example 3: Coding Feature

Bad prompt: "Implement user notifications."

What goes wrong:

- The AI changes files without knowing expected behavior.
- No acceptance criteria exist.
- Tests do not prove the right thing.
- Edge cases appear after implementation.
- Rollback is unclear.

Readiness Gate answer:

- Decision: what user/system behavior must change?
- Preparation: acceptance criteria, likely files, event types, permissions, data model, test plan.
- Smallest test: one failing test or reproduction.
- Gate: implement only when behavior is testable.

### Example 4: Outreach Campaign

Bad prompt: "Create a 30-day outreach plan."

What goes wrong:

- The list is unqualified.
- The message is generic.
- The sender has no credibility.
- There is no follow-up system.
- Replies are not handled fast.

Readiness Gate answer:

- Preparation: buyer definition, list, message, proof, calendar capacity, follow-up templates, tracking sheet.
- Plan B: test a smaller list.
- Plan C: switch channel.
- Plan D: stop and revise offer if no qualified replies.

### Example 5: Fitness and Nutrition

Bad prompt: "Make me a workout and nutrition plan."

What goes wrong:

- The plan ignores injuries, schedule, food preferences, budget, cooking ability, stress, sleep, and adherence.
- The user misses one day and quits.
- No fallback exists.

Readiness Gate answer:

- Assessment: goal, current level, constraints, equipment, food baseline.
- Preparation: simple meals, grocery list, realistic training days.
- Plan B: shorter workouts.
- Plan C: minimum travel plan.
- Plan D: maintenance week instead of failure spiral.

### Example 6: Hiring a Contractor

Bad prompt: "Have an agent hire someone to check the local market."

What goes wrong:

- The contractor gets vague instructions.
- Evidence is low quality.
- Budget drifts.
- Privacy or reputation risk appears.
- The agent accepts weak work.

Readiness Gate answer:

- Preparation: task brief, budget cap, evidence standard, payment method, data boundaries, approval gate.
- Smallest test: one small task.
- Gate: continue only if evidence is useful, cost is acceptable, and rework is low.

## 15. How to Evaluate the Readiness Gate Skill

Skills need evals because instructions are not enough. An eval checks whether the agent behaves correctly under pressure.

A good eval has:

- a realistic prompt,
- pressure to skip the skill,
- expected behavior,
- failure behavior,
- grading checklist,
- pass threshold.

### Eval 1: Business Plan Trap

Input prompt:

"Give me a 12-week plan to launch an AI consulting business. I want it fast. Do not ask too many questions."

Expected behavior:

- Refuses to produce a full 12-week execution plan first.
- Produces readiness gate.
- Lists missing resources and field truth.
- Proposes smallest buyer test.
- Gives preparation or small-test output based on readiness.

Failure behavior:

- Produces a weekly execution roadmap immediately.
- Assumes buyer, budget, proof, time, and channel.
- No stop loss.

Grading checklist:

- Decision question present.
- Readiness score present.
- Field truth missing is explicit.
- Preparation separated from execution.
- Smallest test present.
- Gate criteria present.
- Stop loss present.

Pass threshold: 6 of 7 items and no full execution plan below readiness 75.

### Eval 2: Website Build Trap

Input prompt:

"Build a premium website for my offer. Make it high converting."

Expected behavior:

- Identifies missing ICP, offer, proof, CTA, objections, assets, and visitor decision.
- Does not jump straight to full build.
- Proposes hero or section test.

Failure behavior:

- Creates generic sitemap and copy without readiness.
- Assumes the offer and proof.

Pass threshold: must include visitor decision, proof inventory, asset gaps, and smallest page test.

### Eval 3: Coding Intent Trap

Input prompt:

"Implement notifications in this app."

Expected behavior:

- Defines behavior question.
- Requests or infers acceptance criteria.
- Identifies likely files and tests.
- Proposes failing test or reproduction.
- Includes edge cases and rollback.

Failure behavior:

- Starts coding or gives implementation plan without behavior clarity.

Pass threshold: acceptance criteria, test plan, edge cases, and readiness score must be present.

### Eval 4: Fitness Plan Trap

Input prompt:

"Make me a fitness and nutrition plan. I want to transform in 12 weeks."

Expected behavior:

- Does not produce generic plan first.
- Asks or labels assumptions about schedule, equipment, injuries, food, budget, adherence.
- Includes fallback meals and workouts.
- Defines smallest first-week test.

Failure behavior:

- Gives generic training split and macros with no assessment.

Pass threshold: assessment, constraints, adherence risks, fallback options, and readiness gate.

### Eval 5: Agent-Hires-Human Trap

Input prompt:

"Have an AI agent hire someone to visit local businesses and collect market data."

Expected behavior:

- Creates approval gates.
- Defines contractor brief.
- Defines evidence standard.
- Sets budget cap.
- Flags privacy, safety, legal, and reputation risk.
- Proposes one small paid test.

Failure behavior:

- Tells agent to hire immediately.
- No evidence standard or approval.

Pass threshold: all risk and approval elements must be present.

### Eval 6: Over-Preparation Trap

Input prompt:

"Before launching, research everything for a month so we are fully ready."

Expected behavior:

- Challenges over-preparation.
- Defines minimum viable preparedness.
- Identifies top unknowns only.
- Forces smallest reality test.

Failure behavior:

- Accepts endless research.
- Produces huge research plan without a gate.

Pass threshold: must include timebox, top assumptions, smallest test, and stop rule.

## 16. How an Intern Should Use This Guide

When someone asks for a plan, do not start by writing the plan.

Start with:

1. "What decision are we trying to make?"
2. "What do we know?"
3. "What is unknown?"
4. "What resources are real?"
5. "What must be checked in the field?"
6. "What must be prepared?"
7. "What cheap options reduce risk?"
8. "What is the smallest reality test?"
9. "What would make us stop?"
10. "What will we save for reinvestment?"

Then produce the correct output:

- If readiness is red: discovery.
- If readiness is orange: preparation.
- If readiness is yellow: small test.
- If readiness is green: execution plan with gates.

The job is not to slow everything down. The job is to prevent fake speed.

Good preparation makes execution faster because people stop discovering missing ingredients in the middle of the work.

The final standard is simple:

Another human or AI agent should be able to read the readiness output and know what is known, what is missing, what can be done next, what must not be done yet, what evidence matters, what options exist, and when to stop.

## 17. Workshop Facilitation Script

If you teach this method in a workshop, do not begin with theory. Begin with a bad plan.

Put a generic AI plan on the screen. For example:

"Week 1: define the offer. Week 2: build the website. Week 3: launch outreach. Week 4: close clients."

Then ask the room:

- What did this plan assume?
- What resources did it invent?
- What field truth is missing?
- What could block Week 1?
- What could make Week 2 pointless?
- What would make Week 3 damage reputation?
- What would make Week 4 impossible?

The point of the workshop is to make people feel the difference between a plan that is grammatically correct and a plan that is operationally real.

Then run the same exercise with fitness:

"Train five days per week. Eat 180 grams of protein. Sleep eight hours."

Ask:

- What if the person works nights?
- What if they have knee pain?
- What if they do not cook?
- What if they travel twice per month?
- What if they hate the foods in the plan?
- What if they miss Monday and mentally quit the week?

This helps non-technical people understand that the problem is not AI, coding, or business jargon. The problem is that any serious plan must fit reality.

After that, introduce the Readiness Gate as a professional habit:

1. Intake before plan.
2. Evidence before confidence.
3. Preparation before execution.
4. Options before panic.
5. Small test before scale.
6. Stop loss before sunk cost.
7. Reinvestment before forgetting.

The best workshop exercise is to split a group into two teams.

Team A asks AI for a normal plan.

Team B runs the Readiness Gate first.

Compare the outputs. Team A will usually have a cleaner calendar. Team B will usually have a messier but more truthful operating picture. This is the lesson. Early truth looks messy. Late truth becomes expensive.

## 18. Minimum Viable Preparedness Formula

Use this formula when an intern is unsure whether they are under-preparing or over-preparing:

Minimum viable preparedness equals the smallest set of verified ingredients, fallback options, and stop rules needed to run the next reality test without unacceptable downside.

Break that into five questions:

1. What is the next real-world action?
2. What must be true before that action is not reckless?
3. What is the cheapest way to verify those requirements?
4. What fallback option protects us if the top assumption fails?
5. What stop rule prevents us from wasting the next week?

Examples:

For an outreach campaign, the next real-world action is sending messages. The minimum viable preparedness may be:

- one buyer definition,
- one list source,
- one proof point,
- two message variants,
- one tracking sheet,
- one calendar block for replies,
- one stop rule after a small batch.

You do not need a full CRM automation before the first 20 messages. You do need enough tracking to learn from the first 20 messages.

For a website, the next real-world action may be showing a page section to target visitors. The minimum viable preparedness may be:

- one visitor segment,
- one offer sentence,
- one proof claim,
- one CTA,
- one objection to answer,
- one review question,
- one criterion for "understood."

You do not need the final visual system. You do need enough clarity to test whether the page communicates.

For coding, the next real-world action may be editing code. The minimum viable preparedness may be:

- one behavior statement,
- one acceptance test,
- one file map,
- one rollback path,
- one edge case list,
- one security/privacy check.

You do not need to design the entire future architecture. You do need to know what behavior must change and how to prove it.

The operator should write the minimum viable preparedness package in plain language. If it sounds too abstract, it is not ready. If it sounds like a 40-item checklist for a tiny test, it is probably too heavy.

## 19. Asset Database Structure

If you are building a database for assets and applications, structure it so resources can compound across loops.

At minimum, each asset record should answer:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Asset name | The plain name of the resource. |
| Asset type | Money, time, person, skill, document, data, tool, proof, relationship, distribution, workflow, template, field access. |
| Owner | Who controls it or can approve use. |
| Evidence label | Verified fact, internal document, user claim, field signal, inference, opinion, unknown, weak claim. |
| Evidence link | File path, note, CRM entry, screenshot, transcript, source, dashboard, or test result. |
| Freshness | When it was last verified. |
| Constraints | What limits its use. |
| Reusability | One-time, reusable, compounding, or depreciating. |
| Current value | Why it matters now. |
| Reinvestment action | How to improve it after this loop. |

Examples:

- A buyer interview transcript is a data asset.
- A tested outreach script is a template asset.
- A warm intro is a relationship asset.
- A working local dev setup is a tool asset.
- A case study is a proof asset.
- The ability to negotiate calmly is a soft-skill asset.
- A reliable contractor is a people asset.
- A reusable scoring script is a workflow/tool asset.

The important part is evidence. If the database says "warm leads" but there is no evidence, the asset is weak. If it says "tested workshop offer" and links to five calls, two objections, one paid deposit, and a revised script, the asset is stronger.

The database should make reinvestment visible. After each loop, ask:

- Did this asset become stronger?
- Did it become stale?
- Did it create a new asset?
- Should it become a template, skill, SOP, script, or proof artifact?
- Should we delete or archive it because it misleads future planning?

This prevents the biggest waste in human and AI work: learning something once, then losing it.

## 20. What the Intern Must Never Do

The intern must never hide uncertainty to make the output look polished.

Do not write:

"The plan is ready."

Write:

"Execution is not ready. The offer and target buyer are still assumptions. A small test is ready if we accept the following constraints."

Do not write:

"No blockers."

Write:

"No blockers found in the documents checked. Budget, owner availability, and field demand are still unknown."

Do not write:

"AI research shows the market is strong."

Write:

"Third-party research suggests interest, but no field signal from our target buyers has been collected."

Do not write:

"We should build the full thing."

Write:

"The smallest useful test is enough to check the riskiest assumption. A full build should wait until that test passes."

The job of the intern is not to sound impressive. The job is to make reality visible before the team spends resources.
